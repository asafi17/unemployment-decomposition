Introduction

The Department of Labor releases initial and continuing unemployment insurance (UI) claims data every Thursday at 8:30 a.m. Eastern, covering the week that ended the previous Saturday for initial claims and the week before that for continuing claims. Three features make claims data attractive as a real-time signal relative to the monthly Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) employment situation release:

  1. Frequency. Released weekly which provides more detailed snapshots of the labor market in the periods between the monthly BLS report.

  2. Timeliness. Published with a five-day lag, versus the three-week lag of the BLS payroll report enabling closer-to-real-time updates on the health of the labor market.

  3. Administrative grounding. Claims are counted when people file, not estimated from a survey.

UI programs are designed to provide unemployment benefits to eligible workers who become unemployed through no fault of their own and meet other eligibility requirements. For this reason, the majority of initial claims for unemployment insurance benefits are filed because of a separation from a job the filer did not initiate. Causes of such separations are layoffs, temporary furloughs, completed temporary contracts, among others (see How Do I File for Unemployment Insurance). Because of UI program design, initial claims are a noisy, lower-bound read on separations. Specifically, initial claims will miss separations that do not generate claims such as short-tenure workers, part-timers, gig/1099 workers and people who do not satisfy work-history or monetary-based eligibility requirements.

Continuing claims are the number of people currently drawing unemployment insurance benefits and can change for two reasons. The first is that the number of people who initiate claims changes (i.e., changes to the inflows to the insured-unemployment pool). The second is that people stop claiming benefits (i.e., changes to outflows from the insured-unemployment pool). Broadly, there are three reasons why people stop claiming: They find a job; they exhaust the UI benefits available to them; they fail to maintain eligibility.

Because of these desirable features, UI claims became the default “nowcast” input for labor-market watchers long before high-frequency alternatives existed. However, the labor market signal UI claims provide depends on who the unemployed are. When the unemployed pool is composed of groups UI programs were not designed to cover, the same claims release can imply something very different about the state of the labor market. In this post, we use the Current Population Survey’s (CPS) unemployment-by-cause data to understand the historical and statistical relationship between the composition of the unemployed pool, UI claims, and the unemployment rate.

Why People are Unemployed

The CPS provides six mutually exclusive and exhaustive reason-for-unemployment categories: temporary layoff, permanent job loss, completed temporary job, voluntary quit, reentry to the labor force, and new entry. The definitions of those categories are provided in Table 1.

These categories map onto UI eligibility in very different ways. Temporary layoffs and permanent job losers are the program’s target group: people who have separated from their job for no fault of their own, with filers who typically have the work history and earnings needed to qualify. The remaining four groups are variously outside the program’s design. Completed temp jobs can go either way depending on state rules. Voluntary quits usually don’t qualify except in narrow circumstances. Reentrants often fail work-history tests because their last job was too long ago. New entrants have no prior job at all, so they cannot meet the eligibility criteria by construction.

To see which reason-for-unemployment categories are most plausibly served by UI versus most plausibly missed by it, we construct a profile of each cause. For every cause category in Table 1, we identify the dominant demographic group and compute a representation ratio on a set of standard dimensions: age, education, sex, race, nativity, citizenship, and prior class of worker. The representation ratio is defined as:

\[ \text{representation ratio} = \frac{\text{group's share of this cause's unemployed pool}}{\text{group's share of the overall labor force}} \]

A value of 1 means the group’s presence among that cause’s unemployed is exactly proportional to its presence in the labor force. A value above 1 means the group is overrepresented; a value below 1 means underrepresented. Values are computed on the pooled CPS sample (1994–present) so that each ratio reflects a long-run average rather than a single-year snapshot. We present each cause’s ratios on a radar (“spiderweb”) plot. Each spoke is one demographic dimension, labeled at the end with the dominant group on that dimension (e.g., “Age 16–24” on the age spoke, “High school or less” on the education spoke). The radius at that spoke is the group’s representation ratio. The solid ring at 1 marks proportional representation. A polygon stretching far outside the 1 ring on many spokes says the cause is demographically distinct from the labor force at large. A polygon hugging the 1 ring says the cause’s unemployed look roughly like the country’s workers.

plot_dominant_radar_ratio <- function(ratio_df, cause_name, subtitle, max_ratio = 3.0) {
  plot_df <- ratio_df |>
    filter(cause == cause_name) |>
    arrange(dimension) |>
    mutate(axis_id = row_number())

  n_axes <- nrow(plot_df)
  angles <- pi / 2 - 2 * pi * (seq_len(n_axes) - 1) / n_axes
  radial_breaks <- c(0, 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, max_ratio)

  point_df <- plot_df |>
    mutate(
      angle = angles,
      radius = pmin(ratio, max_ratio) / max_ratio,
      x = radius * cos(angle),
      y = radius * sin(angle)
    )

  closed_df <- bind_rows(point_df, slice(point_df, 1))

  spoke_df <- tibble(
    axis_id = seq_len(n_axes),
    angle = angles,
    x = 0, y = 0,
    xend = cos(angle), yend = sin(angle)
  )

  ring_df <- map_dfr(radial_breaks, \(ring) {
    tibble(
      ring = ring,
      ring_radius = ring / max_ratio,
      axis_id = c(seq_len(n_axes), 1L),
      angle = angles[c(seq_len(n_axes), 1L)],
      x = (ring / max_ratio) * cos(angle),
      y = (ring / max_ratio) * sin(angle)
    )
  })

  axis_label_df <- point_df |>
    transmute(
      dimension, angle,
      label_x = 1.17 * cos(angle),
      label_y = 1.17 * sin(angle),
      hjust = case_when(label_x > 0.15 ~ 0, label_x < -0.15 ~ 1, TRUE ~ 0.5),
      vjust = case_when(label_y > 0.15 ~ 0, label_y < -0.15 ~ 1, TRUE ~ 0.5)
    )

  point_label_df <- point_df |>
    transmute(
      axis_id, angle,
      point_label = label,
      label_radius = case_when(
        radius >= 0.72 ~ pmax(radius - 0.14, 0.16),
        TRUE ~ pmin(radius + 0.11, 0.88)
      ),
      tangent_shift = if_else(axis_id %% 2 == 0, 0.045, -0.045),
      label_x = label_radius * cos(angle) + tangent_shift * (-sin(angle)),
      label_y = label_radius * sin(angle) + tangent_shift * cos(angle),
      hjust = case_when(label_x > 0.18 ~ 0, label_x < -0.18 ~ 1, TRUE ~ 0.5),
      vjust = case_when(label_y > 0.18 ~ 0, label_y < -0.18 ~ 1, TRUE ~ 0.5)
    )

  radial_label_df <- tibble(
    ring = radial_breaks,
    x = radial_breaks / max_ratio,
    y = 0.02,
    label = paste0(scales::number(radial_breaks, accuracy = 0.1), "x")
  )

  ggplot() +
    geom_path(
      data = filter(ring_df, abs(ring - 1.0) > 0.01),
      aes(x = x, y = y, group = ring),
      color = "grey72", linewidth = 0.15
    ) +
    geom_path(
      data = filter(ring_df, abs(ring - 1.0) <= 0.01),
      aes(x = x, y = y, group = ring),
      color = "grey40", linewidth = 0.6, linetype = "solid"
    ) +
    geom_segment(
      data = spoke_df,
      aes(x = x, y = y, xend = xend, yend = yend),
      color = "grey76", linewidth = 0.15
    ) +
    geom_polygon(
      data = closed_df,
      aes(x = x, y = y),
      fill = scales::alpha(cause_cols[[cause_name]], 0.45),
      color = cause_cols[[cause_name]],
      linewidth = 0.15, linejoin = "round"
    ) +
    geom_point(
      data = point_df,
      aes(x = x, y = y),
      color = cause_cols[[cause_name]],
      fill = cause_cols[[cause_name]],
      shape = 21, stroke = 0.2, size = 1.4
    ) +
    geom_text(
      data = point_label_df,
      aes(x = label_x, y = label_y, label = point_label, hjust = hjust, vjust = vjust),
      size = 2.7, lineheight = 0.92,
      color = cause_cols[[cause_name]]
    ) +
    geom_text(
      data = axis_label_df,
      aes(x = label_x, y = label_y, label = dimension, hjust = hjust, vjust = vjust),
      size = 3.6, lineheight = 0.9
    ) +
    geom_text(
      data = radial_label_df,
      aes(x = x, y = y, label = label),
      color = "grey65", size = 3.3, hjust = 0.5, vjust = 0
    ) +
    coord_equal(clip = "off") +
    xlim(-1.28, 1.28) + ylim(-1.26, 1.28) +
    labs(title = cause_name, subtitle = subtitle, x = NULL, y = NULL) +
    theme_report +
    theme(
      axis.text = element_blank(),
      axis.ticks = element_blank(),
      axis.line = element_blank(),
      panel.grid = element_blank(),
      plot.margin = margin(20, 35, 20, 35)
    )
}

For five of the six categories, the polygons hug the 1-ring, deviating on at most one spoke. Composition and UI Takeup

The CPS cause-categories appear to be useful proxies for UI eligibility in the unemployed pool. To test whether that distinction shows up in the UI claims data, we estimate a simple national monthly regression of initial and continuing claims intensity on the composition of the unemployed:

\[ \text{Claims}_t = \alpha + \sum_{j \neq \text{Completed Temp Job}} \beta_j C_j + \gamma_m + \varepsilon_t \]

The outcome is continuing claims scaled by covered employment; the regressors are the five cause shares, with Completed Temp Job omitted as the reference category. Month-of-year fixed effects \(\gamma_m\) absorb seasonality. The sample is monthly national data from 1994 to present, excluding the pandemic window (March 2020 - December 2021) where both compositions and claiming broke from their historical relationships.

extract_share_terms <- function(model, outcome_label) {
  summary(model)$coefficients |>
    as.data.frame() |>

The coefficients align with our expectations. Temporary Layoff and Permanent Job Losers - the two causes closest to UI’s target population - enter with positive signs. Months when these categories make up a larger share of the unemployed see higher continuing claims intensity. New Entrants and Job Leavers enter negatively, consistent with the belief that these groups are likely to be less eligible for UI programs. However, these estimates are not causal. Composition shares are jointly determined with claims by the same underlying labor-market trends.

Two things stand out. First, the New Entrant share is counter-cyclical: it falls in recessions, because permanent job losers and temporary layoffs spike and dilute the entrant share, and drifts back up in expansions. Second, the 12-month average has sat above its long-run mean (~8.6%) since 2022, and averaged ~10.7% over the last twelve months which is notably higher than the pre-pandemic norm (~8.8%). This counter-cyclicality of New Entrants (e.g., UI-ineligible persons) among the unemployed is what makes today’s read of claims data complicated. The fraction of the unemployed that UI claims data miss is widest exactly when the labor market is strong. But a high New Entrants share of the unemployed can arise for two very different reasons, and they imply very different things about the value of the claims signal. The first is the familiar cyclical pattern. In a healthy expansion, separations fall, the unemployed pool shrinks, and new entrants make up a bigger share of what is left. In this world, claims are low because separations are low, and claims are blind to a bigger fraction of the unemployed because more of them are ineligible. An onlooker examining this labor market could reasonably conclude that it is healthy.

The second reason paints a less rosy picture. The New Entrant share could also rise because finding a job has gotten harder for reasons due to weaker hiring, business uncertainty, automation eating entry-level roles or some combination. In that world, new entrants comprise the unemployed pool not because separations have fallen, but because employers aren’t hiring them. Separations stay low, so claims stay low. Composition reports “lots of entrants”, which under the historical patterns would read as a signal of a strong market. But the real story is that the labor market is weakening on the hiring side, and the weakening is concentrated in groups claims cannot see. When that happens, the claims signal could be misleading.

The Flow-Consistent Rate of Unemployment

This is where it helps to bring in the flow-consistent framework used in the Chicago Fed Labor Market Indicators. Following Shimer (2005, 2012) the flow-consistent rate of unemployment is defined as

\[ FCR = \frac{s}{s+f} \]

where \(s\) is the separation rate into unemployment and \(f\) is the job-finding rate out of unemployment. Decomposing the unemployment rate between January 2017 to December 2025 into contributions from \(s\) and \(f\) we find that most of the variation in the monthly unemployment rate is driven by contributions from the hiring rate not the separations rate. This is consistent with Shimer (2005, 2012) in which it was reported that 75% of unemployment rate was driven by contributions from the finding rate and 25% was driven by separations rate.
